# Channels beyond the website

## LinkedIn

**Posts** [`linkedinPost`]. Brief, personal, specific. Each post shares one thing: something built, a problem solved, a decision made or a lesson. Not every post promotes something.

- The first line carries the point, because only about the first 140 characters show before "see more". Make it a concrete statement, not a teaser or a question you then answer.
- Then three to six short paragraphs, 80 to 250 words in all. Include at least one concrete detail: the decision, the tradeoff, an artifact, or a number a source holds.
- End with the takeaway, or a plain pointer to the full work ("The case study has the details:" plus the link). One link at most.
- No hashtag walls (three at most, usually none), no emoji bullets, no "Thrilled to announce", no engagement bait ("Agree?", "Comment YES"), and not one sentence per line for the whole post.
- Write as Miles, first person. Tag or name collaborators who did part of the work.

**Profile**

- Headline [`linkedinHeadline`]: the guide's reusable headline, "Designer & Builder | Brand, Product & Technology", unless Miles asks for a variant.
- About: first person, built from the guide's short bio. Two or three short paragraphs: what he does and believes, the kind of work that proves it (named projects), and what he is looking for only if he confirms it is public (see [Career direction](#career-direction-in-public-copy)).
- Experience entries follow the CV rules below.

## YouTube

Calm, friendly, visually demonstrative, builder to builder. Show the actual work and the practical decisions. Prioritize clarity and retention without hype, and never dunk on other tools.

- **Title** [`youtubeTitle`]: what the viewer will see or learn, specific, under about 70 characters. No clickbait, no all caps, no "you won't believe".
- **Description** [`youtubeDescription`]: the first line (about 150 characters show) says what the video shows and who it helps. Then the links (case study, post, repo if public), then chapters starting at `00:00`. Name tools after the problem they solved.
- **Script** [`youtubeScript`]:
  1. Cold open on the result, under 30 seconds: show it working.
  2. The problem and who has it.
  3. The work, in the order the decisions were made. Mark what is on screen as `[SCREEN: ...]` and let it carry the detail the narration doesn't need to say.
  4. What didn't work, and why the final approach won.
  5. The lesson, and where to see more.
- Say plainly where AI wrote code or content and what Miles directed, reviewed and fixed. Builders watching will ask.
- **Thumbnail text**: two to four words, readable at phone size.

## CV, applications and interviews

Emphasize hands-on design, systems thinking, execution, collaboration and business impact. Tailor the evidence to each role. Never exaggerate engineering depth.

**CV bullets** [`cvBullet`]

- Shape: strong verb, what, for whom or at what scope, then the result a source holds or what shipped. One or two lines, under about 30 words.
- Never open with "Responsible for", "Helped" or "Worked on": they hide what Miles did.
- Job titles and dates exactly as held. When unknown, ask; never infer them from a case study.
- Building claims stay honest about method: "Designed and built X on Next.js and Payload, using AI-assisted development; owned product, design and technical direction" rather than "Engineered a scalable full-stack platform".
- Match the CV's headline to the actual job (product designer, design engineer, design systems, creative technologist), not an assumed specialty, while the summary still reads as a designer who builds.

**Cover letters and application answers** [`coverLetter`]

- Open with why this product and team, specifically. Generic praise of a company reads as a template.
- One or two evidence stories, each in the guide's order: problem, insight, choice, what was built, outcome.
- Explain the move from running an agency to contributing deeply to one product as a deliberate choice: more focus, deeper ownership over time. State it once, plainly, without apologizing for the agency years.
- 250 to 400 words. Close plainly; no "I would be thrilled".

**Interview answers**

- The same narrative order, spoken: 60 to 120 seconds (about 150 to 300 words). Include the tradeoff and the lesson; interviewers probe both.
- Give real numbers only when a source holds them; otherwise describe the change in concrete terms.
- Prepare the leadership story so it shows judgment without eclipsing the maker: what Miles decided, and what he made with his own hands.

## Bios

- One line: the guide's headline.
- Short (first person, about 40 words): the guide's short bio as written.
- Long (about 100 words): the short bio, plus named evidence from the pillars that fit the audience, plus Brooklyn. Third person ("Miles Roxas is...") for speaker pages, event programs and press; first person for his own profiles.

## Career direction in public copy

The guide records that Miles is seeking a full-time, hands-on role at a product organization and moving to a silent-partner role at Suits & Sandals. That is direction, not yet history. Use it freely in applications, cover letters and interview prep. In public copy (LinkedIn, the website, YouTube), say it only when Miles confirms it for that piece, and never describe the agency transition as done until he says it is.
